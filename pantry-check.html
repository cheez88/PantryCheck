    How the score works

    Every food gets up to 100 points from three parts. Nutrition counts most, because it’s the best guide to what a food does for you. Processing still counts for a lot.

    Nutrition (60 points) uses the 2023/24 Nutri-Score, which penalises sugar, saturated fat, salt and kilojoules and rewards fibre, protein and fruit and vegetables. The app adds three rules of its own: points off for added sugar found in the ingredients (Australian labels only show total sugar), the same sweetener penalty for foods that Nutri-Score uses for drinks, no credit for protein or fibre added to sugary products, and an allowance for plain cheese, because Nutri-Score doesn’t count its calcium.

    Processing (30 points) uses the NOVA system. Whole foods get 30 and processed foods 21. Ultra-processed foods get 14 with one sign of ultra-processing or 10 with more, with extra points off for sweetened drinks and processed meats, and some back for high-fibre, low-sugar bread and cereal and for unsweetened yoghurt. Cooking ingredients like sugar, butter and oil aren’t scored.

    Additives (10 points) loses 4 for each additive of high concern, like nitrites in processed meat, 2 for moderate concern, 1 for low concern, and 1 for each additive beyond three (up to 2). Harmless ones like citric acid and pectin cost nothing.

    Limits. Confectionery (sugar-free included) and soft drinks never score above 39, and nor does anything with a Nutri-Score E. Biscuits, cakes, chips, processed meats and cereals with more than 15g of sugar per 100g stop at 59. To be an everyday food, a product needs a Nutri-Score of A or B, no additives of high concern and a full ingredient list.

    75 and above is an everyday food, 40 to 74 a sometimes food, and below 40 an occasional food. Products without a nutrition panel aren’t scored until you add the label details.

    The weights and limits come from a review of the evidence in October 2026. They’re judgement calls, so treat scores as a guide.

    If a barcode isn't in the database, you can paste the label details from a shopping website, or photograph the label. Claude reads the nutrition panel and ingredients, you check the numbers, and the result is saved on your phone for next time.

    Product data comes from Open Food Facts, a free community database. Scores are a guide for making better choices, not medical advice.
